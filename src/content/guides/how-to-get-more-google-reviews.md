I once asked a hair salon owner in Point Loma why she had only 11 Google reviews after eight years in business. Her answer: "It feels like begging."

I get it. Nobody wants to be that business that hands you a card with five stars printed on it. But here's the thing: your happy customers *want* to help you. They just don't think about it. Your job isn't to beg. It's to make it easy and to ask at the right moment.

That salon went from 11 reviews to 94 in about six months, using the exact scripts below.

## Why reviews matter so much now

- In BrightLocal's 2026 consumer survey, **97% of consumers** said they read reviews for local businesses.
- Reviews are one of the biggest factors in whether you show up on Google Maps.
- AI tools like ChatGPT and Google's AI lean heavily on reviews when they recommend businesses. A review that says "she was amazing with my curly hair" helps you show up when someone asks for a stylist who's good with curly hair.

## The golden rule: ask at the happy moment

The best time to ask is right after the customer feels good. The haircut looks great. The AC is blowing cold. The dog came home fluffy and smelling like a cookie. Not three weeks later in a newsletter.

## Step 1: Get your review link

Log in to your Google Business Profile and look for the option to ask for reviews or share your review link. Copy that link. It takes people straight to the review box, which removes most of the friction.

Pro tip: put that link in a QR code at your counter, too.

## Step 2: Use these scripts

### In person (at the counter or end of the job)

> "I'm really glad you're happy with it. Can I ask a small favor? We're a small business, and Google reviews make a huge difference for us. I'll text you a link. If you have a minute later, we'd really appreciate it."

### Text message (send within a few hours)

> Hi [Name], it's [Your name] from [Business]. Thanks again for coming in today! If you have 60 seconds, a quick Google review would mean a lot to our small team: [link]. And if anything wasn't perfect, reply here and tell me first. I'd like to fix it.

### Email (for appointment-based businesses)

> **Subject:** Quick favor from [Business]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Thanks for choosing us for [service]. We're a small, local team, and reviews are one of the main ways new customers find us.
>
> If you have a minute, would you share a few words about your experience? It's helpful when people mention what you came in for and what stood out.
>
> [Leave a review button]
>
> Thanks so much,
> [Your name]

Notice the line about mentioning what they came in for. That's what gets you specific reviews, which help both customers and AI tools understand what you're good at.

### The follow-up (one time only)

> Hi [Name], just a friendly nudge on the review link from last week, no pressure at all. [link] Thanks again for trusting us with [service]!

One follow-up. That's it. More than that starts to feel like begging, and we promised not to beg.

## Step 3: Make it a habit, not a campaign

Reviews from 2021 don't help much. Recent reviews tell Google and customers you're still great. Pick one person and one moment:

- The front desk sends the text after checkout
- The tech sends it when they close the job
- The owner sends it every Friday to that week's customers

The salon assigned it to whoever did the checkout. Every client got a text within an hour. That consistency is what got them to 94.

## Step 4: Reply to every review

Good reviews: thank them by name and mention something specific. "Thanks, Jamie! So glad the balayage came out exactly how you pictured."

Bad reviews: stay calm, don't argue, take it offline. "I'm sorry this happened, Mark. That's not the experience we want anyone to have. Please call me directly at [number] so I can make it right."

Future customers read your replies as much as the reviews. A gracious reply to a bad review can win you more business than a five-star review.

## What not to do (Google's rules, plain English)

- **Don't offer anything for reviews.** No discounts, freebies, or contest entries. Google doesn't allow incentivized reviews.
- **Don't only ask happy customers.** Filtering who gets asked based on whether they'll leave a good review, sometimes called review gating, isn't allowed. Ask everyone.
- **Don't write fake reviews or ask employees and family to.** It's against the rules, and it can get reviews removed or your profile flagged.
- **Don't buy reviews.** Ever.

## Starting from almost nothing (the cold-start problem)

If you have 5 reviews and your competitors have 200, don't panic — and don't try to close the gap in a week. The move is a sprint, then a habit:

1. **List the last 20 customers you can still reach.** Old clients, recent jobs, regulars. The first ten reviews are the hardest; you likely have them sitting in your contacts already.
2. **Ask in batches, not bursts.** Ten asks this week, ten next week. A steady drip looks natural; 50 in one day looks bought.
3. **Make it effortless.** Send the link with the ask. Every extra step (find us on Google, click around, log in) loses people. The review link from your profile goes straight to the form.
4. **Never write it for them.** "Here's what to say" crosses the line. Reminding them what you did ("the Tuesday drain repair") is fine and often enough.

After the sprint, the habit takes over: every happy moment gets an ask, and the number grows on its own.

## What to do when a review you earned never shows up

It happens, and it's usually not about you:

- **Google's filter is aggressive.** New accounts, single-review accounts, and bursts of reviews get held. Ask the customer to try again in a few days.
- **The customer may have posted on the wrong platform** — check Yelp, Facebook, and any industry directories before assuming it's lost.
- **Review counts lag.** Sometimes a review appears on the profile but not in the count for a while.
- **If reviews from the same person keep vanishing,** don't fight it. Move on to the next customer.

One missing review is noise. The system works on volume and recency, not any single post.

## What to write back (templates that sound human)

Replying to reviews is one of the few free things that clearly helps, and most businesses skip it. The bar is low: sound like a person, not a form letter.

- **5-star review:** "Thank you — this made our week. Glad the [specific thing they mentioned] went smoothly. We appreciate you taking the time."
- **4-star review:** "Thanks for the kind words — and for the honest feedback on [what they mentioned]. That's genuinely useful. If there's anything we could have done to earn that fifth star, we'd love to hear it."
- **Negative review:** "We're sorry about your experience — that's not how we want to do business. We'd like to make it right. Please reach out to [contact] and ask for [you]."
- **Obvious fake or spam:** report it to Google, and don't argue with it publicly. A calm, professional reply for future readers is still worth writing.

Two rules for all replies: never get defensive, and remember you're writing for the *next* customer who's reading, not the reviewer.

## FAQ

**How many Google reviews does a small business need?**
There's no magic number. Look at your top competitors on Google Maps and aim to match or beat them, then keep reviews coming steadily.

**Can I offer a discount for a Google review?**
No. Google doesn't allow incentives for reviews.

**Can I ask for a review by text message?**
Yes. Text is often the most effective way, as long as the customer gave you their number and expects to hear from you.

**What should I do about a fake negative review?**
Reply calmly and professionally, then report it through your Google Business Profile if it violates Google's policies.

**How do I get more reviews without violating Google's policy?**
Ask every customer, at the happy moment, without offering anything in return. That's it — that's the whole policy in practice. What gets you in trouble is incentives, review gating (only asking happy customers), buying reviews, or writing reviews yourself. The honest ask, done consistently, is both allowed and the thing that actually works.

**Is it OK to ask customers in person?**
It's the best way. People say yes far more often in person than by text or email, and there's no question of whether the ask was genuine. Hand them your phone with the review form open, or send the link on the spot. The ask should take under a minute.

**A customer says they left a review but it's not showing. What do I do?**
First, check on your profile directly rather than trusting the count. If it's genuinely missing, ask them to try again in a few days — Google holds back reviews from new or one-review accounts. Don't ask them to repost repeatedly; a few attempts is the limit.

**Should I respond to every review?**
Every single one, including the glowing ones. Replies are read by future customers and signal to Google that the profile is active. Short and human beats long and corporate. Templates are fine as a starting point — just vary the details so they don't read like copy-paste.

**How often should I ask for reviews?**
Every happy moment, forever. There's no quota where you should stop. The businesses that dominate their market ask after every single job — that's the entire difference between 20 reviews and 200.
